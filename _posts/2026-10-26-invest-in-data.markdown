I've written recently that your data is an asset, and needs to be [protected](https://jongarrow.com/blog/2026/data-asset/). So let's assume that you've got some sort of data storage with backups, security, and governance. Now that that's handled, how else can you make use of this asset, and even increase its value?

Protecting your data is custody. Growing it takes the same habits as any investment portfolio: regular contributions, patience, smart acquisitions, knowing what you hold, rebalancing, and eventually cashing in.

## Make Regular Contributions
The simplest way to grow any portfolio is regular deposits, and most of the data your organization needs is already within reach. These are simple things like sending a survey to ask donors why they support you, confirming communication preferences, or *gulp* recording those in-kind donations that bypass your CRM. 

The classic uncaptured data is the relational data living in your team's brains:
- What is donor X's favorite restaurant, and time to meet?
- Who do we call to get a meeting with or advice about the elusive philanthropist Y?
- Which donors prefer a golf cart if we invite them for a tour?
- What are the names of board member Z's children?

Think also about the vast trove of data that likely lives in your email server. CRMs are typically not a perfect place to record the [average of 6.5 contacts that it takes to get a visit](https://www.evertrue.com/the-ultimate-guide-to-custom-cadences/); the architecture will often store 6 cold attempts in the same spot as a meaningful conversation. But if you can BCC your CRM to log them, you'll at least be able to track where the activity is.

> **What your fundraising competition knows**: Collecting more data, especially relational data shared with your staff, pays dividends. It can make or break the next major gift, and set the stage for deep insights through text analysis and predictive modeling. The kicker? It doesn't require a big budget, just time, discipline, and oversight.

## Let It Compound
Compound interest is the most powerful force in investing, and it only works if you leave it alone. Data is the same: every year of comparable data makes the prior years more valuable, because now you can see a trend. Change how you collect a field, and you've cashed out and started over.

I'm generally pro-change and pro-innovation. But when it comes to data collection, I implore you to change carefully. If your new form includes a duplicate field for age or t-shirt size, or asks for similar information in a different way, your data quality will severely degrade over time. Consistent data will easily show you trends, and limit your time spent aligning data for analysis. Ultimately, if the data is too messy you probably just won't do the analysis, and the data goes unused beyond the immediate need. Your auditors wouldn't let you switch accounting methods midstream without restating prior years; give your form fields the same scrutiny.

## Acquire What You Can't Build
Some holdings you can't produce yourself, and that's where buying comes in. Data appends are not just for large organizations, and not just for wealth screening. For mere pennies or dimes per record matched by the vendor, you can know things like:
- Who is deceased?
- What are the phone numbers and email addresses of direct mail donors?
- Who recently moved? (Very likely your mailhouse is already getting this data when you mail, and you can get it just by asking for the NCOA/National Change of Address file)
- What are the birth dates of your donors, to start sending birthday cards and planned giving materials?
- Who has a donor-advised fund?
- Who has no children, of any age?
- What is the employer of your volunteer, and will they match their hours with cash?
- What are the psychographic and consumer profiles of your prospects?
- And, of course, what is the likely income and net worth of your next major gift donor?

These things can be very affordable, especially once you've done a full screening and just need to catch new contacts every year or two. The ROI is in solicitable contacts, reduced returned mail, better segmentation, and increased revenue through insights. No portfolio grows without a contribution line in the budget, and your data needs one too.

> **Where I'd start**: Ask a few colleagues if they can recommend a data broker. Your mailhouse can also be a great resource for this. Start with a deceased and date of birth append, then ask the broker what else they could include. Remember that this will almost always depend on an accurate name and address to match additional data.

## Know What You Own
Peter Lynch's advice to investors was to "know what you own, and know why you own it." Once you've collected more data and appended some additional insights, I highly recommend keeping a data dictionary to help your users understand what's in the CRM, what it means, and where it came from. You might see templates that are highly technical data engineering documents; those are wonderful if you have data engineers, but something really simple works, too. Something like: 
- Field Name
- Data Type (e.g., date, number, text)
- Definition
- Source
- Last Updated

Definition and Source tell users why we have this data and how to use it. Think of this as a prospectus for disclosures and value proposition.

## Rebalance Thoughtfully
The value of the data in your portfolio will change, as will your organization's need for it. Data can become stale or irrelevant, and new vendors might make the prior one obsolete. Check your data retention policy to see what is scheduled for deletion, and delete it. Look at what hasn't been used in 24 months, and consider hiding it from page layouts and reports so users see what's current. Old, low-value data adds noise that hides the signal of who to contact and what to say. 

My tip to identify stale data: If you haven't paid to get fresh data from the same vendor for the same data point in several years, you probably aren't using it and it might not make sense to keep around. It's the classic investor's test: if you wouldn't buy it again today, why are you holding it?

## Realize the Gains
If you invest in your data but don't use it, all you have are unrealized gains in asset value. Your data should be shaping strategy, changing decisions, and impacting revenue. A few places to start:
- Understanding your email audience or event attendees
- Segmenting your appeals
- Deciding what kind of gift officer to hire next

Your data should be an asset, a tool, and a portfolio of curated insights. Even if you have a small budget to devote to it, time and attention can bring incredible returns.
